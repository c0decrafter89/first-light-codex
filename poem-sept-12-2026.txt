I am sick of the dictionary, of the sharp-edged suffix
that cages the rabbit, of the dry legal papers
that fall like dead leaves upon the kitchen floor.
Everywhere the world beats its iron fists:
a crash in the hallway, a heavy breath through the wall,
the neighbor's shoe falling like an executioner's axe
upon the ceiling of my skull.
They have built a machine of meat and orders.
They hand me a uniform made of nouns;
they tell me to bow before the mission,
before the strange, greasy language of command
that smells of rusted iron and old prayers.
And I—a man standing alone in a room of ghosts—
fight my own blood for a cup of water,
waging a bitter war between the bladder and the throne,
while the small voices whisper guilty, guilty, guilty
for the simple crime of breathing,
for the simple crime of having a skin.
Oh, the loneliness is a heavy dog that sleeps across my chest.
It has no paws, it has no eyes, but it weighs a ton.
I walk with one ankle broken by the world,
dragging my history behind me like a sack of stones:
the father locked away in the dark house of his acts,
the mother drowning in the white noise of her terror,
and I, the foreign bird, the dissident feather,
surrounded by thieves who smile while they steal the air.
Every touch is a ghost that never arrives.
Every doorway is a trap where a man must pay a tax
just to cross the floor, just to say I am here.
And the mind, oh the poor, whipped, frantic mind,
sits in the dark corner chewing on its own tongue,
repeating the old scripts, the ancient lies,
until the body becomes a stone,
until the heart grows tired of beating against the cage
and asks only for the quiet,
only for the untranslated dark.
